---
title: "How This Guide Verifies Mushroom Matcha Claims"
description: "What counts as a source for a caffeine number, a comparison, or a mushroom health claim in this guide, and what does not."
---

Mushroom matcha content online tends to repeat two kinds of unsourced claims: caffeine numbers stated with false precision (or, just as often, a false "low-caffeine" label that doesn't apply to matcha), and mushroom health benefits stated as settled fact. This guide handles both carefully.

## Caffeine numbers

Caffeine content in tea varies by cultivar, harvest, leaf maturity, water temperature, steep or whisk strength, and how much powder is used per cup. No single number is correct for every cup of matcha. Where this guide states a caffeine figure, it is presented as a **commonly cited range** (30-70mg per 8oz cup for matcha), sourced from tea-industry and food-composition references, not a lab result specific to any one product.

## Why matcha is not a low-caffeine drink

This is the single most important correction this guide makes relative to generic "mushroom tea" content online. Matcha uses the youngest, shade-grown leaf buds, the highest-caffeine part of the tea plant, consumed whole as a powder rather than steeped and strained. That combination puts matcha's caffeine content above standard green tea and well above roasted teas like hojicha. Any page in this guide that discusses caffeine states this plainly rather than defaulting to a generic "tea is gentler than coffee" framing that doesn't hold for matcha specifically.

## Mushroom health claims

Lion's Mane, Cordyceps, and Reishi appear throughout this guide in the context of mushroom matcha. Claims about them follow these rules:

1. **Traditional-use and preliminary-research language is labeled as such.** Phrases like "traditionally used for" or "early research suggests" are used deliberately, not as a softer way of stating a settled medical fact.
2. **No disease-treatment or cure claims.** This guide does not state or imply that any mushroom treats, cures, or prevents a disease. That is a medical claim, and it is not one this guide, or the FDA, allows a supplement or food product to make.
3. **Human clinical evidence is distinguished from animal or in-vitro studies.** Where the underlying research was done in mice, rats, or a lab dish rather than in people, that's stated, since it changes how much weight the finding should carry.

## Where a claim is about Mushroom Matcha specifically

Wherever this guide cites a specific serving figure for Mushroom Matcha itself, such as its stated 28 servings per 1oz bag or its 1g-per-serving specification, that number comes from the brand's own product page at mushroommatcha.us, not from independent lab testing. It's labeled as the brand's own stated specification everywhere it appears. A brand's own marketing copy, including Mushroom Matcha's, does not count as an independent source for its own health claims.

## What does not count as a source

- A blog post that restates a caffeine number with no citation
- A "10 health benefits of X mushroom" article that doesn't distinguish traditional use from clinical evidence
- Marketing copy from a mushroom supplement brand, including Mushroom Matcha's own, treated as an independent source for its own claims
- Generic "mushroom tea is low caffeine" framing applied to matcha without accounting for matcha's actual caffeine profile

## Where the underlying facts come from

Botanical and processing information (cultivar, shade-growing, stone-milling) draws on established Japanese tea industry references. Caffeine ranges draw on published food-composition data for green tea and matcha specifically. Mushroom claims draw on peer-reviewed research where available, with the study type (human, animal, in-vitro) stated alongside the finding.
